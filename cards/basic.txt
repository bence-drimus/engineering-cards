eng:basic:threat-model/what	What is a threat model, and why start a security design with one?	A short list of what you're protecting and who from. It tells you which defences matter, so effort goes where the risk is.	lens::security topic::threat-model
eng:basic:threat-model/who	Apart from outside attackers, what threats belong in a small system's threat model?	Someone holding one stolen secret, your own misconfiguration, and plain bugs.	lens::security topic::threat-model
eng:basic:cia-triad/confidentiality	In the CIA triad, what does confidentiality guard against?	Data being read by someone who shouldn't see it.	lens::security topic::cia-triad
eng:basic:cia-triad/integrity	In the CIA triad, what does integrity guard against?	Data or commands being changed or forged without anyone noticing.	lens::security topic::cia-triad
eng:basic:cia-triad/availability	In the CIA triad, what does availability guard against?	The system being knocked over or made unreachable when it's needed.	lens::security lens::reliability topic::cia-triad
eng:basic:outbound-tunnel/what	How can a server take web requests without opening any inbound port?	With an outbound tunnel: a connector on the server dials out to the provider's edge and keeps the connection open, and requests come back down it.	lens::security topic::outbound-tunnel
eng:basic:outbound-tunnel/attack-surface	Why does an outbound tunnel shrink a server's attack surface?	Nothing listens on a public port, so scanners have nothing to connect to. The only way in is through the provider's edge, where you can put authentication.	lens::security topic::outbound-tunnel
eng:basic:outbound-tunnel/cloudflared	What does Cloudflare Tunnel's connector, <code>cloudflared</code>, connect to?	Cloudflare's edge on port 7844, over QUIC (UDP), with TCP as a fallback.	lens::security topic::outbound-tunnel
eng:basic:outbound-tunnel/trade-off	Compared with a reverse proxy on an open port 443, what does a tunnel cost you?	Every request goes through the provider's edge, so you depend on it staying up and on trusting it with your traffic.	lens::security lens::reliability topic::outbound-tunnel
eng:basic:threat-model/shared-host	An app sits behind a tunnel with no open port. Why might its server still be exposed?	Other services on the same host can still listen publicly, such as a web server on 80/443 or SSH on 22. The tunnel only covers the app behind it.	lens::security topic::threat-model
eng:basic:stateful-firewall/what	What makes a firewall stateful?	It tracks connections, so replies on a connection opened from inside are let back in without a rule of their own.	lens::security topic::stateful-firewall
eng:basic:stateful-firewall/tunnel	Why does an outbound tunnel need no inbound firewall rule?	Outbound connections are allowed by default, and a stateful firewall lets the replies on them back in. Requests travel inside that connection.	lens::security topic::stateful-firewall
eng:basic:stateful-firewall/docker-ports	In Docker Compose, who can reach a service that has no <code>ports:</code> entry?	Only other containers on the same compose network. Nothing is published on the host.	lens::security topic::stateful-firewall topic::docker
eng:basic:authn-authz/difference	What's the difference between authentication and authorisation?	Authentication asks who you are. Authorisation asks what you're allowed to do.	lens::security topic::authn-authz
eng:basic:authn-authz/example	A service token proves which client is calling, and that client is refused the admin endpoints. Which part is authentication and which is authorisation?	Proving which client it is, is authentication. Refusing it the admin endpoints is authorisation.	lens::security topic::authn-authz
eng:basic:authn-authz/least-privilege	What is the principle of least privilege?	Give each identity only the permissions its job needs, so a stolen credential can do as little as possible.	lens::security topic::authn-authz
eng:basic:authn-authz/edge-auth	What do you gain by putting authentication at the edge, for example with Cloudflare Access, instead of building a login?	Requests are checked before they reach the app, and there's no users table, password hashing, session store or reset flow of your own to get wrong.	lens::security topic::authn-authz
eng:basic:edge-login/oauth	You log in through an identity-aware proxy with GitHub. Why does the proxy never see your password?	It's OAuth: you type the password into GitHub, and the proxy only gets back who you are.	lens::security topic::edge-login
eng:basic:edge-login/jwt-cookie	After the OAuth login, what does an identity-aware proxy like Cloudflare Access give the browser?	A JWT it has signed, kept as a cookie (<code>CF_Authorization</code> for Access) until it expires.	lens::security topic::edge-login
eng:basic:edge-login/each-request	On each later request, what does the proxy check, and how does the app learn who's calling?	The proxy checks the cookie's signature and expiry, then forwards the request with the identity in a header (<code>Cf-Access-Jwt-Assertion</code> for Access) that the app reads.	lens::security topic::edge-login
eng:basic:edge-login/expiry	The proxy's session expires but you're still logged in to the identity provider. What happens?	One tap: the proxy sends you back through the provider, which signs you straight in and the proxy issues a new token.	lens::security topic::edge-login
eng:basic:stateless-sessions/what	What makes a session token stateless?	The token carries everything needed to trust it, so checking it is a signature and an expiry with no session lookup.	lens::security lens::performance topic::stateless-sessions
eng:basic:stateless-sessions/vs-stateful	How does a stateful session differ from a JWT session?	The cookie holds only a random id, and the server looks the session up in a store on every request. Logging someone out is deleting the row.	lens::security topic::stateless-sessions
eng:basic:stateless-sessions/revocation	What's the main trade-off of stateless session tokens?	You can't revoke one before it expires without adding state back, such as a denylist checked on each request.	lens::security topic::stateless-sessions
eng:basic:jwt-header/standards	Which standards define a JWT, its signature and the published signing keys?	JWT is RFC 7519, the signature format (JWS) is RFC 7515 and the key set (JWKS) is RFC 7517.	lens::security topic::jwt
eng:basic:jwt-header/vendor-header	Is <code>Cf-Access-Jwt-Assertion</code> part of the JWT standard?	No. The JWT inside is standard, but the header name is Cloudflare's own. Google IAP uses <code>x-goog-iap-jwt-assertion</code> and AWS ALB uses <code>x-amzn-oidc-data</code>.	lens::security topic::jwt
eng:basic:jwt-header/bearer	What's the standard header for a client to send a token in?	<code>Authorization: Bearer &lt;token&gt;</code>, from the OAuth 2.0 bearer token spec (RFC 6750).	lens::security topic::jwt
eng:basic:defence-in-depth/what	What is defence in depth?	Layering independent controls, so one of them failing doesn't expose the system on its own.	lens::security topic::defence-in-depth
eng:basic:defence-in-depth/verify-jwt	Why verify the proxy's JWT in the app when the proxy has already checked the login?	If a hostname is ever routed without an access policy, anyone can send that header. Checking the signature against the published keys means a forged one gets nothing.	lens::security topic::defence-in-depth topic::jwt
eng:basic:defence-in-depth/aud	What does checking a JWT's <code>aud</code> claim protect against?	A valid token issued for a different application being replayed against yours.	lens::security topic::defence-in-depth topic::jwt
eng:basic:defence-in-depth/cost	What does defence in depth cost?	More parts to build, configure and keep in step, such as fetching signing keys and keeping the expected audience right in the app.	lens::security lens::operability topic::defence-in-depth
eng:basic:blast-radius/what	What does blast radius mean in security design?	How much damage one failure or one leaked secret can do. Good design keeps it small.	lens::security topic::blast-radius
eng:basic:blast-radius/per-client	Why give each client its own credential instead of one shared secret?	A leak can be revoked for that client alone, and the logs show which client it was.	lens::security topic::blast-radius
eng:basic:blast-radius/binding	Each client has its own token. What stops a stolen token posing as a different client?	The server maps each token to the client it belongs to and refuses any request where it claims to be someone else.	lens::security topic::blast-radius
eng:basic:blast-radius/client-id	In a service token's client id and client secret, which half is secret?	Only the client secret. The client id is like a username: it names the caller and is fine to keep in config.	lens::security topic::blast-radius
eng:basic:end-to-end-integrity/what	What is end-to-end integrity?	The receiver checks a signature made by the sender itself, so it trusts the message rather than the network path or the servers in between.	lens::security topic::end-to-end-integrity
eng:basic:end-to-end-integrity/compromised-path	A server signs the commands it sends and clients verify them. What can a compromised database, DNS or proxy do?	It can't make a client act, as it can't forge a signature without the private key. It can still delay or drop messages.	lens::security topic::end-to-end-integrity
eng:basic:end-to-end-integrity/limit	What's the limit of a server signing commands for its clients?	The server holds the private key, so if the server itself is compromised the attacker can sign anything.	lens::security topic::end-to-end-integrity
eng:basic:end-to-end-integrity/rotation	When should you rotate a signing key even with no known leak?	When you leave a platform that held it, since you can no longer say who could read it.	lens::security lens::operability topic::end-to-end-integrity
eng:basic:end-to-end-integrity/ed25519	Why is Ed25519 a common choice for signing?	Small keys and signatures, fast to verify, and hard to misuse because signing needs no fresh random number.	lens::security topic::end-to-end-integrity
eng:basic:csrf/what	What is CSRF?	Cross-site request forgery: a malicious page makes your browser send a request to a site you're logged in to, riding on your cookie.	lens::security topic::csrf
eng:basic:csrf/why-cookies	Why does CSRF work against cookie sessions but not against a token sent in a header?	The browser attaches cookies to cross-site requests by itself. A header token has to be added by the site's own script, which another site can't run.	lens::security topic::csrf
eng:basic:csrf/sec-fetch-site	How does the <code>Sec-Fetch-Site</code> header defend against CSRF?	The browser sets it to say where a request came from, and a page can't change it. Refusing requests unless it's <code>same-origin</code> blocks other sites.	lens::security topic::csrf
eng:basic:csrf/samesite	Which cookie attribute also limits CSRF, and how?	<code>SameSite</code>: set to Lax or Strict, the browser leaves the cookie off most cross-site requests.	lens::security topic::csrf
